Disclosure Day - AI's takeover of Steam

Like I mentioned in the previous article on Vampire Survivors and its clones, the recent high profile issue of Needle in a Haystack Simulator being cloned using AI to steal territory is a growing problem on Steam. A major complaint with Steam Next Fest was AI-generated copies of many games in the event were being promoted the same as the games they were based on, with the customer left to do the homework on whether or not the one they're looking at is the original.

This is a problem that is only growing, and Valve's stance on the matter so far is to remain somewhat hands-off, though they have indicated they are aware of it. Whether or not this is the right approach won't be debated here, but the trend is highly likely to accelerate until something changes. As a temporary measure, Valve ask developers to put in a voluntary disclosure over whether or not AI was used in the development of your game.

This disclosure is required simply for two categories: Pre-generated content (art/code/sound/etc) created with the help of AI tools during development and Live-Generated content (AI-generated content provided while playing the game). Which tools are used is not required. The generalised nature of this disclosure means it's impossible to discriminate between a game that was developed with AI used as a minor part of one of its workflows versus a game that was entirely generated with AI. Both will be flagged just the same.

I would like to start by saying this is purely an analysis of the growing trend of games on Steam where AI-generated content was used in development. There is a meatier piece of text at the end that will open things up for discussion, but in the interests of getting to the data first, I wanted to kick off with a single idea and take it from there.


AI is going nowhere.

We do hear about the imminent AI bubble bursting all the time, but this is in relation to the big AI companies that are pushing it everywhere they can. The truth is the technology is now so deeply integrated with everything in the world that, even if a bunch of these trillion-dollar companies collapse and fail, AI is here to stay. That's something we have to figure out how to navigate in the future.

But for the moment, let's talk about some games, shall we?

Share of releases with an AI disclosure by month

Back in June 2024 was when Valve began requiring developers to voluntarily disclose whether or not AI was used in the making of games. There were some games before that point that did disclose this information, but you can see immediate jump in the trend and the gradual linear increase. By June, more than 10% of games had an AI disclosure.

October 2026 is when the "flippening" happened, a point by which more new releases than not are disclosing AI use in development (54.3%). It's possible the true flip happened sooner, given that some developers likely do not disclose AI use.

It was highlighted on a recent Aftermath podcast that the consequence of not declaring AI usage and later being discovered to have done so allows Valve to forcefully refund buyers.

There are essentially two kinds of free games on Steam - the passion projects and art games folk want to make because it's important to them, and the games that allow you no barrier to entry, but have microtransactions. What's interesting about this chart is it tells us developers who use AI are more interested in getting paid than ones who don't.

Games by initial price (€)

As for the paid games, the ones with AI disclosure statements generally occupy the lower end of the price scale. Whether or not this translates to the lower end of the value scale is up for debate. What is definite is how the median price for an AI disclosure game is cheaper than its median non-AI counterpart by 20c (€4.99 vs €5.19)

Looking at the same data as a % of All Releases, it's clearer to see that the games with AI disclosure hover around multiples of 5 the same way the the AI Disclosure do. But as a percentage of the whole, AI disclosure games have spikes at every price point below and including €8. With the notable exception of €0, where you are less than half as likely to find one compared to games released in the non-AI disclosure category

Top 100 tags on games with an AI disclosure

Above is a list of the most common tags attached to the games with an AI disclosure. Singleplayer, Indie, Action, Adventure, 2D, 3D, and Casual have all been removed as they are common and nearly ubiquitous to both categories.

Looking at the biggest difference in % of all releases. There are notably more games tagged with Simulation, strategy, Management, Economy, Idler, and Incremental that have AI disclosure. A lot of these games feature complicated and nebulous systems where an AI can "close the circle" in terms of everything that's required to make the game complete.

There is an unsurprising large gap when it comes to Roguelites, since the genre by design often has somewhat restrained level design. Focusing less on the play area than the game's mechanics, putting players in a randomly generated arena based on a few templates is easier and faster to get a model to do than have a curated, hand-crafted series of levels.

Platformer is an outlier here, being the most common tag that favours non-AI disclosure. This is possibly down to the fact most game engines have a platformer template that developers can piggyback off to make their own game, and a large number of online game developer courses are of the ilk "make your own platformer". Along with the fact platformers are one of the simpler game types out there, some combination of these circumstances likely explains this anomaly.

Though surprising at first glance, the relatively large difference when it comes to Immersive Sim and Choices Matter is probably explained by how these types of games (that normally require a large amount of curation) are given more room to "breathe" through the generation of on-the-fly outcomes. Normally these types of games would be highly curated in their design, but using AI-generated content to assist interactions could conceivably lower the barrier to making one of these.

Where tags sit, AI disclosure and no AI disclosure

Each point is one of the hundred tags attached to the most games with an AI disclosure. Across is the share of games with no AI disclosure that carry it. Up is the share of games with an AI disclosure that carry it. Singleplayer, Indie, Action, Adventure, 2D, 3D, and Casual are left out. They are by far the most common tags.

Another way of looking at the same tag data shows how far off the expected mean some of these tags are.

Searching for Simulation-Economy games in the last 3 years, the top game by review count is BidKing, which has mostly negative reviews, due to the large amount pf AI-generated content and unreliable servers, leading to a rarely-seen scale of disapproval on Steam.

Even some of the better reviewed titles in this category, like Survival Log, have reviews criticising its use of AI for generating dialogue and narrative text, as well as its soundtrack.

Words in titles of games with an AI disclosure

Looking at the most common words in the games made with AI disclosure, an uninspiring picture begins to form. Many of the words are generic placeholders that begin to explain why a Steam user might consider AI-developed games "slop".

You can take almost any two words from the cloud, plug them into Steam search, and discover a multitude of games that have 10 or fewer reviews, developed with AI, and are little more than a concept that has been pushed to the store. As an example, take Idle Tower Clicker and you'll see a game developed with AI, released at the tail end of October, with No user reviews, no community interaction, and has a price point under €5. This is prototypical of the kind of AI-associated growth that is encroaching on the platform.

But are AI-disclosed games any use?

The trillion-dollar question. On a recent Game Business podcast interview with Romy Halfweeg of Poki, Halfweeg and host Christopher Dring discussed the idea that just because a game is made with or without AI doesn't actually determine whether a game is good. It's possible to make a good game using AI and also to make a terrible game organically and by hand-crafting every single element. There is no absolute.

However, just because it is not absolute fact doesn't mean it's uncommon, something that becomes plainly obvious when looking at the overall share of review scores. Putting aside the low/no scores for a moment, Games with no AI-disclosure outperform games with AI disclosure in every neutral-to-positive category.

Negative reviews do take up a larger share on the No AI disclosure side too, though these are somewhat marginal in the grand scheme of things, taking up < 1.5% of all games released in either category. While negative reviews are not a rarity on Steam, pile ons are indeed rarer than one might expect.

Of course, the most damning thing here is that over 2/3rds (67% at the time of writing) of all games with AI disclosures have 10 or fewer reviews. That is a quite astonishing amount of abandonware, junk, slop, or whatever you want to call it. These games are being sent out to die. Or perhaps just occupy server space. The vast majority of them have zero engagement with a community, Steam as a whole, or the Internet in general. They appear to have no purpose, and it is now having a non-negligible having an effect on the platform.

Positive review score percentage of games with an AI disclosure, by month

Stream graph of games with an AI disclosure, by review percentage. Ribbons are counts, displaced around the center of the axis.

This chart doesn't actually use Steam's pre-defined categories, instead it delivers a percentage score for each game based on the number of positive reviews divided by the total number of reviews. Finally, we see the problem laid bare. AI-assisted games are taking up a large and growing amount of real estate in gaming's premier marketplace. Users are pretty good at identifying these games, hence the reason the majority of them come with 10 or fewer reviews.

What this shows is customers are pretty smart at identifying low-effort games, and these are the games where most people don't even both to play or review, yet the numbers of these games are still increasing at an incredible rate.

The interesting thing I find here is how the number of games with AI disclosures that score well (or indeed very well) are not rapidly increasing over time. If you take any point from January 2025 onward, you generally get between 100-150 games scoring 70% or better, with one or two exceptions.

The problem here is the barrier to entry has been completely smashed and, when that happens, the market gets saturated by games are more than just low-effort, they have no sense of community or legacy.

Putting it all together

Each point is a game with more than 10 positive reviews and a known list price. Gold is a title with AI disclosure, Cyan is no disclosure. The three axes are Tag-score (the likelihood that a game is AI-developed based on its tags), List price (the price the game was initially listed for in €), and Count of Positive Reviews.

Here we really see the whole picture, and it shows the AI games tend to cluster toward the octant closest to the origin.

The Tag-Score axis assigned a likelihood score of a game being AI-developed based on the tags used in the store page.

The tags from the chart above survive the 10-review cutoff. Simulation is on 41.1% of disclosed games with more than 10 reviews and 22.7% of the rest. Management, economy, idler, and incremental lean the same way. Platformer still leans the other way, 3.9% against 9.7%.

Shoutout to Fishy2 for being the game least likely to be AI generated in the entire Steam catalogue with a tag-score of -23.8.

Similarly: shoutout to Bubble Tea Revolution for being the game least likely to be AI generated in the entire Steam catalogue a score of 100, with its AI capsule art, AI in-game images and audio, AI translations, etc...

As you probably guessed from the name of the site, I believe game discovery is a crucial and hard thing to accomplish, and has been for the last 10 years at least. What we're seeing here with the advent of low-effort AI-developed games spreading on Steam like some sort of zombie apocalypse is just another threat to that, and not one that's easy to solve.

I do not believe AI is fundamentally bad or evil as a technology. There are ethical issues around how models have been trained and how corporations wield it, but its capacity for good cannot be ignored. In the right hands, it can be a great tool for change, positivity, and utility.

Unfortunately for us, what we're seeing here is AI (mostly) in the wrong hands. We now live in an age where you can open any IDE with any LLM attached, type "Make me a game", and Claude, Gemini, or even Grok will give you a pretty basic working model of a game in any genre. And once it does that, there's not a huge amount between exporting the game from wherever you're building it and uploading it to a storefront.

One knock-on effect of this is games being submitted for review on Steam are now taking weeks to pass through their QA testing rather than days. I personally know one developer whose game's release date had to be pushed back from next week to early December because of the swathe of games currently being submitted. Bear in mind that Steam's QA process has nothing to do with the quality of the game, just that it can run and that it passes some security checks.

Also, Valve are unlikely to do anything about this if it doesn't affect their bottom line. Sure, it's a pain for users, and they are testing out things like the Personal Release Calendar. But that won't preclude these low-score, low-effort, low-engagement games from being recommended at some point. And with Valve's gentle policy that they "don't want to be kingmakers", it seems unlikely they will do anything to prevent this growing influx of titles.

Worse still, as models get better, it'll become even easier for someone to prompt "Make me a copy of X game" and it will recreate it in full, allowing them to put their own branding on it and submit it to the store, as we've seen in the recent Needle in a Haystack Simulator affair.

However, there is room for optimism. One thing we've already established is how customers are savvy and, though a pain to navigate through this swamp, they will do it to get to the games that are worth their time. They will avoid games with no community, no marketing, no hype, no reviews. They will avoid the games with AI capsule art, even if it's not declared.

But they will also end up avoiding all games with an AI disclosure. There already exist mods for the store that will obfuscate or hide games from search results that have declared to have been developed with the assistance of AI. This is regrettably deeply unfair to the developers who make quality games and are transparent in their AI usage who will get punished instead of the copycat developers who vibe-code their whole games and are too lazy to even bother putting in an AI disclosure.

Some people will avoid anything involved with AI on moral grounds, and that is a reasonable position to take, though it will become exponentially more difficult in the coming years. I suspect the vast majority won't care about it, as long as the product is decent. But really there must be a solution that works for everyone.

In the coming weeks and months, I hope to build something approaching that solution on Game Discovery Tool. I know this isn't something that will ever be "fixed", but now that the flood has begun, it's time to put out the sandbags.
